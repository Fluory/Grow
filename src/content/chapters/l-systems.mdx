The plants in this garden are not drawn. They are *derived* – from small grammars called **Lindenmayer systems**, invented in 1968 by the biologist Aristid Lindenmayer to describe how algae and plants grow.

## A string that rewrites itself

An L-system starts with a short string, the *axiom*, and a set of rewriting rules. `F` means "draw a branch segment", `[` and `]` save and restore the position of a small turtle, `+`, `&` and `/` turn it in three dimensions, `L` places a leaf cluster. And `A` is an *apex* – a growing tip that the rules replace in every generation.

Every `A` became a fork with two or three new branches, each ending in a new `A`. The string grows exponentially – after five rewrites the oak has several hundred segments.

## Stochastic, so no two trees are alike

The rules are *stochastic*: an apex forks into two branches in some cases and three in others, and every angle is jittered a little. The random numbers come from a seed derived from the plant's id, so the same plant always grows into the same tree – on every computer, in the SVG and in 3D – while two oaks never look the same.

## Growth levels: a new ring of branches per rain day

The trick that makes the garden *grow* is that every module remembers the generation it was born in. Because only apices are rewritten, generation *n + 1* always contains generation *n*. So a plant can be shown at any **level** between zero and its last generation: segments born up to that level are visible, the newest ones grow in from the tip they sprang from, and the leaf tuft at the old tip shrinks away.

## One skeleton, two pictures

The turtle works in 3D. The README picture is simply the front view of the skeleton; the website builds tapered tubes from the very same segments, sorted by the level they were born in, so a growth level is just a draw range on the GPU. The SVG and the 3D garden can never disagree about a single branch.
